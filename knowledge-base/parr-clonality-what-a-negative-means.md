# A negative PARR does not mean "not lymphoma" — what clonality testing can and cannot rule out in cats

> Generated 2026-07-27 · PubMed search + verbatim verification · Not diagnostic, not a prescription
> **Purpose**: PARR (PCR for antigen receptor rearrangements) is often received by owners — and sometimes discussed — as if it were a verdict. It is not. This file records what the published performance figures actually are in cats, because the asymmetry between a positive and a negative result is large, counter-intuitive, and decision-relevant.
> **Why it was written**: an owner, reading their own pathology reports, noticed that a cytology diagnosis of high-grade lymphoma sat alongside a PARR that found **no clonal band for either T or B cells**, and asked what that combination means. That is precisely the contradiction-detection this project exists to support — and the literature answers it clearly.
> **Division of labour**: subtypes, grading and prognosis are in [`feline-oncology-literature-survey.md`](feline-oncology-literature-survey.md) §3; how *treatment response* is judged in [`response-assessment-and-drug-resistance.md`](response-assessment-and-drug-resistance.md). This file is about **one diagnostic test's error profile**.

## 0. The finding that matters most

### 0.1 A negative PARR is wrong roughly two times in five

In a prospective feline study (20 lymphoma cases, 10 cats without haematopoietic neoplasia):

> *"Diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of the clonality assay were 70% and 90%. Overall diagnostic accuracy was 77%, positive predictive value 93% and **negative predictive value 60%**."* (Hammer 2016, PMID 27813229)

> **⭐ Read the asymmetry, because it is the whole point.**
> **A positive PARR is strong** — positive predictive value **93%**. If it finds a clone, that means something.
> **A negative PARR is weak** — negative predictive value **60%**. Put plainly: **among cats whose PARR came back negative, roughly 40% still had lymphoma.** A negative result is closer to "this test did not detect it" than to "this is not lymphoma."
>
> So a report combining a **cytological diagnosis of high-grade lymphoma** with a **PARR negative for both lineages** is **not a contradiction requiring resolution** — it is one of the most common ways this test behaves. **The morphology is not overturned by the negative.**

### 0.2 The reason is in the detection limit, and it is startling

> *"Detection limits for clonal populations within a polyclonal background was **90% for B-cells and 50% for T-cells**."* (Hammer 2016)

> **For a B-cell clone to be detected, it had to make up ninety percent of the sample.** Any meaningful admixture of reactive lymphocytes, inflammatory cells, blood or stroma can therefore push a genuinely clonal population below the threshold. **A negative PARR is as much a statement about the sample as about the disease** — which is why sampling site, technique and cellularity matter to how much weight the result can carry.

### 0.3 In the setting where it is used most, specificity collapses

PARR's commonest use in cats is separating chronic enteropathy from small-cell alimentary lymphoma. Measured against an expert-panel reference standard in 54 cats, clonality testing had *"sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 85.7%…, **33.3%** …, and 61.5% … relative to the panel decision"* (Marsilio 2020, PMID 32100916).

> ⚠️ **Specificity 33.3% in that setting means a positive result frequently appeared in cats the expert panel called inflammatory.** Note this pushes the *opposite* way from §0.1 — there, a positive was strong. **The test's error profile is not fixed; it depends on the question being asked and the population.** The same study found histology-guided mass spectrometry outperformed it (accuracy 88.9% vs 61.5%), which is why "PARR is the confirmatory test" is itself an assumption worth checking.

## 1. What to do with a negative PARR

- **Do not treat it as excluding lymphoma**, especially where morphology, imaging and clinical course point that way. Negative predictive value 60% is the number to hold.
- **Ask what fraction of the sample was neoplastic.** Given the 90%/50% detection limits, a diluted or low-cellularity sample makes a negative nearly uninformative.
- **Ask which primer sets were used and whether both lineages were tested** — and note that a clone of a lineage the primers do not cover cannot be found by definition.
- **A positive is much more informative than a negative — except in the enteropathy-versus-small-cell setting**, where §0.2's specificity finding cuts the other way. **Which direction the test is trustworthy in depends on the question.**
- **⛔ None of this reclassifies a diagnosis.** PARR is one input among cytology, histopathology, immunohistochemistry, imaging and clinical course, and integrating them is the attending clinician's and pathologist's work. What this file supports is knowing **how much weight one line on the report can bear**.

⚠️ **Not covered here**: what a "high grade / immunoblast-like" morphology combined with a **low mitotic count** implies. No feline study addressing that specific combination was located for this file, so it is recorded as an **open question**, not answered. It is a reasonable thing to put to a pathologist directly.
