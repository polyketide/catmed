# Does adding doxorubicin help a cat? — the COP-versus-CHOP question, and why the dog standard may not transfer

> Generated 2026-07-27 · PubMed search + verbatim verification · Not diagnostic, not a prescription
> **Purpose**: CHOP is the standard of care for canine lymphoma, and the "H" is doxorubicin. Owners and clinicians routinely assume the same holds in cats — and in Chinese-language owner communities, COP is in fact used about **2.3× more often than CHOP** (see the corpus note in `docs/LITERATURE-PIPELINE-SOP.md` §7a). This file asks what the feline evidence actually supports. The claim it was written to check is a common one, stated by an owner as *"COP is right; CHOP may be better in dogs; **in cats there is no evidence at all**."* That claim is **directionally defensible but too strong** — and the exception matters.
> **Division of labour**: doxorubicin's cat-specific toxicity is in [`antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md`](antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md); protocols and survival in [`feline-oncology-literature-survey.md`](feline-oncology-literature-survey.md) §3; how "response" is judged in [`response-assessment-and-drug-resistance.md`](response-assessment-and-drug-resistance.md).

## 0. The finding that matters most

### 0.1 There IS a randomised trial supporting doxorubicin in cats — and it is easy to miss

> *"Thirty-eight cats with lymphoma were treated with vincristine, cyclophosphamide, and prednisone (COP). They were randomized at entry to receive maintenance chemotherapy consisting of either single-agent doxorubicin or continued COP… The median remission duration for 11 cats continuing to receive COP was 83 days, which was **significantly shorter** than for 7 cats that received doxorubicin (**281 days**)."* (Moore 1996, PMID 8947869)

> **⭐ So "no evidence in cats" is not accurate — there is a randomised comparison, and it favoured doxorubicin by more than three-fold in remission duration.** Anyone arguing the COP-only position should know this trial exists, because it is the strongest single piece of evidence against them.
>
> ⚠️ **And now the reasons it does not settle the question.** The randomised comparison rests on **11 versus 7 cats** — eighteen animals. It is from **1996**. And critically, it tested doxorubicin as **maintenance after a COP-induced remission**, not as part of induction — which is *not* the CHOP-versus-COP question as usually posed. **A three-fold difference in eighteen cats from thirty years ago is a signal worth respecting and not a standard worth mandating.**

### 0.2 As a single agent in cats, doxorubicin performs poorly — two independent studies say so

- 19 cats, prospective, doxorubicin alone: **CR 32%, PR 32%, no response 36%**, and the authors' conclusion is unambiguous: *"doxorubicin **cannot be recommended as a single agent** for treatment of feline lymphosarcoma because of the rather poor remission rate achieved"* (Peaston 1999, PMID 10451728).
- 19 cats, retrospective, doxorubicin alone: **8/19 (42%) responded, CR in 5 (26%)**; *"Doxorubicin was **not very effective** at inducing and maintaining remission in the cats in this study"*, with **loss of appetite in 9 cats (47%), severe in 5 (26%)** (Kristal 2001, PMID 11300595).

> **This is the species-transfer point.** In dogs, doxorubicin is the backbone agent. In cats, given alone, it induces complete remission in roughly a quarter to a third — **and the drug's target organ in the cat is the kidney, not the heart** (see [`antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md`](antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md)). An older cat with unrecognised CKD is exactly the patient in whom that matters. **"It is standard in dogs" is not a feline argument.**

### 0.3 COP-based results are not obviously worse

In the only randomised feline trial of the vinca component, 40 cats on **COP-based** chemotherapy achieved **lymphoma-specific survival of 139 vs 136 days** across the two arms (Krick 2012, PMID 23157371). ⚠️ That is not a comparison with CHOP and must not be read as one — different study, population and era. But it sits in the same range as CHOP-based feline series (see [`feline-oncology-literature-survey.md`](feline-oncology-literature-survey.md) §3), **which is why the COP-first position is not eccentric.**

The same trial supplies a separate, practical finding: **cats given vincristine were far more likely to be switched off it for gastrointestinal toxicity than cats given vinblastine — 44.4% vs 10.5% (P = .02)** — with equivalent efficacy. *"Results suggest that vinblastine is a reasonable alternative to vincristine in the treatment of some cats with lymphoma."*

## 1. The finding that speaks to "is any of this worth it"

A 60-cat Australian series treating with sequential multi-agent chemotherapy reported outcomes that are worth stating precisely, because owner communities circulate far bleaker figures:

- **80% complete remission** after the *induction* phase alone: *"48 [of 60] rapidly went into complete remission following the administration of l-asparaginase, vincristine and prednisolone (complete remission rate 80%)"*, with median survival 187 days in that group.
- **20 cats were long-term survivors (>1 year)**, and *"at least 14 were 'cured' based on the absence of physical evidence of lymphosarcoma 2-years after initiating treatment. In other words, **of the 48 cats that reached complete remission, in excess of 29% were 'cured'**"* (Malik 2001, PMID 11837901).

> **⭐ Two things follow, and both matter to how owners talk about this disease.**
> **First, the folk figure is wrong.** A "cure"/completion rate *"around 1%"* circulates in owner communities. Whatever "结疗" is taken to mean, **29% of complete responders showing no evidence of disease at two years is not compatible with 1%**, and neither are the CR rates in [`feline-oncology-literature-survey.md`](feline-oncology-literature-survey.md) §3 (CR 76% low-grade, ORR 85.7% small-cell, 66% high-grade). ⚠️ An owner who believes the treatable fraction is 1% may decline treatment on a number that is off by more than an order of magnitude.
> **Second, early response carries the prognosis.** The authors' own recommendation: *"The decision to embark on chemotherapy should be based on the results of induction chemotherapy with l-asparaginase, vincristine and prednisolone, as the response to this was a good predictor of long-term survival."* **This is the published form of "first remission is the best remission"** — and note that the induction it rests on is L-asparaginase + vincristine + prednisolone, i.e. **no doxorubicin.**

⚠️ **Caveats that travel with these numbers.** 2001, single region, 60 cats; young FeLV-negative cats with mediastinal disease were markedly over-represented among long-term survivors (*"7 of 9 cats less than 4 years-of-age were long-term survivors"*), and that subgroup is not the median owner's cat. **Do not quote 29% as a general cure rate.** Quote it as evidence that the treatable fraction is *substantial*, not 1%.

## 2. At the decision moment

- **The honest summary is neither "CHOP is better" nor "there is no evidence".** There is **one small randomised trial favouring doxorubicin in the maintenance role** (§0.1), **two studies showing it is weak as a single agent** (§0.2), **no modern head-to-head of COP versus CHOP as induction in cats**, and a **cat-specific renal toxicity** that dogs do not share. A clinician who chooses COP first, reserving doxorubicin, is making a defensible reading of that; so is one who includes it.
- **If doxorubicin is being considered, renal function is the gate**, not a formality — see [`antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md`](antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md).
- **If vincristine is causing gastrointestinal toxicity, vinblastine is an evidence-backed swap** at equivalent efficacy (§0.3) — a specific, useful thing to raise.
- **⚠️ Dose-intensified ("high-dose") cyclophosphamide in cats:** no study evaluating this was located for this file. It is **not** recorded here as either supported or unsupported — only as **unexamined by anything this project could retrieve**, which is itself worth saying before a protocol is built on it.
- **⛔ None of this is a protocol recommendation.** Which agents, in which order, at which doses, belong to the attending oncologist, who has the staging, the bloodwork and the cat.

**PMID 8947869** · Moore AS 1996
> Thirty-eight cats with lymphoma were treated with vincristine, cyclophosphamide, and prednisone (COP).
> They were randomized at entry to receive maintenance chemotherapy consisting of either single-agent doxorubicin or continued COP therapy, starting on week 4 of treatment and continuing for 6 months or until relapse.
> The median remission duration for 11 cats continuing to receive COP was 83 days, which was significantly shorter than for 7 cats that received doxorubicin (281 days).

**PMID 10451728** · Peaston AE 1999
> Of 19 cats treated with doxorubicin alone, 6 (32%) had complete remission, 6 (32%) had partial remission and 7 (36%) did not respond.
> The results suggest that doxorubicin cannot be recommended as a single agent for treatment of feline lymphosarcoma because of the rather poor remission rate achieved.

**PMID 11300595** · Kristal O 2001
> Eight of the 19 cats (42%) responded to doxorubicin for a median duration of 64 days (range, 35-575 days).
> Five cats (26%) achieved a complete response (CR) to doxorubicin for a median duration of 92 days (range, 54-575 days).
> Loss of appetite was the most common toxicity, observed in 9 cats (47%), and was severe in 5 cats (26%).
> Doxorubicin was not very effective at inducing and maintaining remission in the cats in this study.

**PMID 23157371** · Krick EL 2012
> Cats in both arms had similar response rates, PFS, and LSS (48 versus 64 days, P = .87; 139 versus 136 days, P = .96).
> Cats that received vincristine were significantly more likely to switch arms based on gastrointestinal toxicity than cats that received vinblastine (44.4 versus 10.5%, P = .02).
> Results suggest that vinblastine is a reasonable alternative to vincristine in the treatment of some cats with lymphoma.

**PMID 11837901** · Malik R 2001
> Of the 60 cats, 48 rapidly went into complete remission following the administration of 1-asparaginase, vincristine and prednisolone (complete remission rate 80%) and these cats had a median survival of 187 days.
> Twenty cats were classed as 'long-term survivors' based on survival time in excess of one year and at least 14 were 'cured' based on the absence of physical evidence of lymphosarcoma 2-years after initiating treatment.
> The decision to embark on chemotherapy should be based on the results of induction chemotherapy with l-asparaginase, vincristine and prednisolone, as the response to this was a good predictor of long-term survival.
> Eighty five percent of owners expressed complete satisfaction with their decision to pursue chemotherapy and 70% believed their cat's health status improved during the first 2-weeks of treatment.
