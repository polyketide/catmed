# Acid suppression in cats — once-daily omeprazole did not beat placebo

> Generated 2026-07-27 · PubMed search + verbatim verification · Not diagnostic, not a prescription
> **Purpose**: omeprazole is among the most commonly given drugs in a vomiting cat, and the single most decision-relevant feline finding about it is about **frequency**, not dose or timing. This file exists because that finding is easy to miss and changes what a given prescription is actually doing.
> **Division of labour**: antiemetics (maropitant, ondansetron) are in [`supportive-and-palliative-care.md`](supportive-and-palliative-care.md) §2; chemotherapy-induced emesis per drug in [`antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md`](antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md). **Acid suppression and antiemesis are different jobs** — see §2.

---

## 0. The finding that matters most

In a randomised four-way crossover in 8 cats, with intragastric pH monitored continuously for 96 hours:

| Treatment | % time pH ≥3 | % time pH ≥4 |
|---|---|---|
| **Omeprazole twice daily** | **67.0 ± 24.0%** | **54.6 ± 26.4%** |
| Omeprazole once daily | 24.4 ± 22.8% | 16.8 ± 19.3% |
| Ranitidine twice daily | 16.5 ± 9.0% | 9.6 ± 5.9% |
| Placebo | 9.4 ± 8.0% | 7.0 ± 6.6% |

> *"Only twice-daily PO administered omeprazole significantly suppressed gastric acidity in healthy cats, whereas **once-daily omeprazole and standard dosages of ranitidine were not effective acid suppressants in cats**."* (Šutalo 2015, PMID 25966746)

> **⭐ Read the once-daily row against the placebo row.** 24.4% versus 9.4% was **not statistically distinguishable from placebo**. A cat on once-daily omeprazole is, on this evidence, **not reliably receiving acid suppression at all** — and ranitidine at standard doses fared no better.
>
> **So the first question about an omeprazole prescription in a cat is not "before or after food" — it is "how many times a day".** That is the variable with a randomised feline trial behind it, and it dwarfs the others.

⚠️ **Limits.** Eight **healthy** cats, one crossover study, enteric-coated granules at 1.1–1.3 mg/kg. It measures **intragastric pH**, not clinical outcomes — it does not show that twice-daily dosing makes a vomiting cat better, only that it actually suppresses acid where once-daily did not. Whether this cat needs acid suppression at all remains the clinician's judgement.

## 1. Timing relative to food — no feline evidence located

Owners often reason that an acid suppressant should work better on an empty stomach. **This file found no feline study of PPI timing relative to feeding**, so the question is recorded as **unanswered**, not answered.

⚠️ **And note the reasoning may run the other way.** Proton-pump inhibitors are prodrugs that bind **actively secreting** pumps, and pump activity is stimulated by feeding — which is why human practice conventionally gives a PPI **shortly before a meal** rather than on a truly empty stomach with no meal following. **This is mechanistic reasoning carried from human pharmacology, explicitly not a feline finding**, and it is recorded here only to show that "empty stomach must be better" is an assumption rather than a safe default. **Ask the prescribing clinician; do not resolve it by intuition in either direction.**

## 2. ⚠️ Acid suppression is not antiemesis — do not substitute one for the other

A cat vomiting on chemotherapy is most often vomiting because of **emetogenic drug effect**, not because of acid. Vincristine in particular is emetogenic — in a randomised comparison **44.4%** of cats required a drug change for gastrointestinal toxicity (see [`antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md`](antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md)).

**The agents with feline randomised evidence for vomiting are antiemetics**: a single subcutaneous dose of maropitant (1 mg/kg) or ondansetron (0.4 mg/kg) controlled vomiting/nausea within 24 h, with no significant difference between them ([`supportive-and-palliative-care.md`](supportive-and-palliative-care.md) §2).

> **⛔ Giving an acid suppressant where an antiemetic is indicated is not a neutral substitution** — it treats a different mechanism and may simply not work. Which one this cat needs is the attending clinician's call.

⚠️ **A practical point that outranks both**: an oral drug given to a cat that has just vomited a large volume may simply be returned, and the attempt itself carries handling stress and, in a compromised cat, aspiration risk. **Whether to give an oral dose at all right now is a question for the clinician, not a timing optimisation.**
