# G-CSF (升白针) in Cats — why repeated *human* G-CSF can cause the neutropenia it is given to fix

> Generated 2026-07-26 · PubMed search + verbatim verification · Not diagnostic, not a prescription
> **Purpose**: this file exists because G-CSF is, by a wide margin, the most-discussed supportive-care intervention among owners of cats on chemotherapy — and the product actually being injected is almost always a **human** recombinant G-CSF (filgrastim; sold in China as 瑞白 / 惠尔血, elsewhere as Neupogen/Zarzio). There is a species-specific trap in that, it is documented, and owners are already half-observing it without knowing what it is.
> **Division of labour with the rest of this knowledge base**: [`antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md`](antineoplastic-drug-toxicity.md) says *which drugs suppress the marrow*; this file is about *what is done afterwards*. It does not cover febrile-neutropenia antibiotics, transfusion (see [`blood-types-and-transfusion-compatibility.md`](blood-types-and-transfusion-compatibility.md)), or anaemia/ESA (see [`supportive-and-palliative-care.md`](supportive-and-palliative-care.md) §10.1).

## 0. The finding that matters most

### 0.1 Every cat given human G-CSF in the controlled trial developed neutralizing antibodies

In a randomized controlled trial comparing feline G-CSF, pegylated feline G-CSF and **human** G-CSF (HuG-CSF) in cats: *"all HuG-CSF-treated cats developed neutralizing antibodies, suggesting cross-reactive antibodies to endogenous G-CSF in a majority of the cases with severe neutropenia"* (Coleman 2014, PMID 24662027). The paper opens by stating the problem plainly: *"long-term HuG-CSF treatment can be associated with adverse effects, including neutropenia."*

> **⭐ Read that mechanism carefully, because it is counter-intuitive and it is the whole point of this file.** The antibodies a cat raises against the *human* protein **cross-react with the cat's own G-CSF**. The drug does not simply stop working — it can leave the cat *worse than before*, unable to mount its own neutrophil response. **A treatment for neutropenia becoming a cause of neutropenia is the specific hazard here**, and it is a function of *repeated* exposure, not of a single course.
>
> The same trial supplies the proof-of-mechanism from the other direction: when species-matched pegylated feline G-CSF was used to rescue cats that had been made neutropenic by human G-CSF, *"clinically normal neutrophil numbers returned."* Independently confirmed in a second line of work: *"repeated administration of hG-CSF induces undesirable anti-drug antibody (ADA) responses"* (Chi 2021, PMID 34509747).

**This is what owners are already seeing without a name for it.** In owner communities the observation circulates as *"some cats stop responding to 瑞白"* and, from an owner whose cat had received many cycles, the fear that *eventually the injections will not raise it any more*. That is the published mechanism, observed from the inside. **A cat that has become refractory to repeated human G-CSF is a documented phenomenon, not bad luck** — and it is a reason to raise species-matched or alternative strategies with the oncologist rather than simply injecting more.

### 0.2 A second, less-known harm: other cell lines can fall

- **Platelets.** *"Two weeks after completion of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor therapy, the cat developed severe thrombocytopenia, with the granulocyte colony-stimulating factor therapy considered to be the most likely cause"* — the first reported feline case, which resolved spontaneously (Kim 2021, PMID 34967142).
- **Red cells and platelets, as a group effect.** In 22 cats given filgrastim, while white cells and neutrophils rose, *"red blood cells, haemoglobin, haematocrit and platelets parameters registered a considerable reduction from day 1 to day 5 with a significant statistical difference (p < 0.01), considered as post-administration side effects"* (Dascalu 2024, PMID 39765486).

**So "升白针" is not a free action.** It raises one line and may depress others — which matters most in exactly the population this file concerns: a cat already cytopenic from chemotherapy.

## 1. What the evidence actually is — and the gap nobody states out loud

⚠️ **Almost all controlled feline filgrastim data is in *panleukopenia (FPV)*, not chemotherapy-induced neutropenia.** Dascalu 2024 (22 cats) and Tüfekçi 2026 (49 cats) are both FPV studies; Coleman 2014 is FIV-infected and healthy cats; Klar 2024 (PMID 38662994) is a **single case report** of azathioprine-induced neutropenia. **The indication owners are using it for — post-chemotherapy neutropenia in a cancer patient — is the one with the least direct feline evidence.** Extrapolation may well be reasonable; it is still extrapolation, and should be named as such rather than assumed.

**And the outcome that matters was not improved.** In the largest of these, comparing low-dose filgrastim, high-dose filgrastim, an immunomodulator and standard care in 49 cats: high-dose filgrastim gave the best haematological recovery, but *"Survival rates did not differ significantly among groups (p = 0.615)"*, and the authors close with an unusually direct caveat — *"the efficacy of filgrastim and/or iPPVO treatments has not been definitively confirmed, likely due to the small sample size and the lack of well-controlled randomized studies"* (Tüfekçi 2026, PMID 41976045). **Counts came up; survival did not measurably change.** ⚠️ These are small studies in a different disease, so this is not evidence that G-CSF fails to help a chemotherapy patient — it is evidence that *nobody has shown it helps*, which is a different and more honest statement.

## 2. Reference doses — published feline values, to check against, NOT to give

They are **not a prescription and not permission to inject.** ⚠️ **Every one comes from a *panleukopenia* study, not from chemotherapy-induced neutropenia** — the indication differs from the one this file is about. ## 3. At the decision moment

- **The question to raise is *how many times*, not *whether it works*.** A single short course and a tenth course are different exposures with different risk profiles (§0.1). If a cat has needed repeated G-CSF, the antibody/refractoriness problem is the specific thing to ask the oncologist about — including whether a species-matched product is obtainable, since species-matched G-CSF avoided neutralizing antibodies in the trial while the human product did not.
- **A normal neutrophil count is not an indication.** G-CSF is given for a documented low count on a current CBC; owner communities occasionally report it being given at normal counts, and peer challenge on that point is appropriate. **The CBC decides, not the schedule.**
- **Watch the other cell lines too, not just the neutrophils** (§0.2) — platelets and red cells fell in the published series.
- **Fever plus neutropenia is an emergency and is not a G-CSF question.** A febrile, neutropenic cat needs to be seen urgently; see [`emergency-triage-red-flags.md`](emergency-triage-red-flags.md). Nothing in this file justifies managing that at home.
- **This file supplies no dosing and no schedule.** That is the attending oncologist's, on a current CBC.

## 4. Febrile neutropenia — when it happens, and why the timing matters to an owner

§3 ends by calling fever-plus-neutropenia an emergency. It does not say **when** it
tends to happen, and that gap costs owners the one inference they can make at home
at 2am: *which injection could this even be?*

The only feline series on this is small (22 events in 20 cats, eight institutions,
retrospective) but it is directly on point.

**Timing.** Median days between chemotherapy administration and onset was **5
(range 4–25)**. So a fever starting **within 24 hours of an injection is outside the
reported window** and points away from that particular dose — while a fever at
day 5–7 sits squarely in it. ⚠️ **The range is wide (4–25 days) and n is small; this
orders suspicion, it does not exclude anything.** Two drugs given a week apart can
both be inside the window at once.

**Which drugs.** Lymphoma was the commonest diagnosis, and **lomustine and vinca
alkaloids** (vincristine, vinblastine) were the most frequently implicated agents.

**What it looked like on presentation.** Median temperature **40.0 °C (range
39.5–40.6)**, median absolute neutrophil count **246** (range 0–1600). Presenting
signs were decreased appetite, lethargy, vomiting and diarrhoea.

⚠️ **Unit note.** The PubMed abstract renders that count as `246 mL`, which is a lost
`µL` — the excerpt below is quoted exactly as the record reads, errors included.
Check the published paper before using the figure.

**Outcome.** All but one cat received intravenous fluids and broad-spectrum
antibiotics; **fevers resolved in all cases** and neutrophil counts returned to
normal in 19. This is treatable — the thing that has to happen is the CBC.

### What this does and does not let an owner conclude

- ✅ It gives a **prior**, not an answer. A fever the day after an injection and a
  fever a week after are not equally likely to be this.
- ❌ It does **not** let anyone rule febrile neutropenia in or out at home. The
  median neutrophil count above is far below any reference range, and **nothing
  visible from outside the cat distinguishes it from a tumour fever or an
  infection.** Only a CBC does.
- ⛔ **A normal CBC from before the fever does not clear the cat.** Counts can fall
  within a day or two, and the pre-treatment sample was drawn before the drug
  that matters was even given.
- ⇒ The output of this section is a **question for the clinic**, never a decision to
  wait: *the cat is febrile at day N after drug X — can we have a CBC with
  differential today?*

**PMID 24662027** · Coleman JK 2014
> Neutropenia can often be corrected by treatment with granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) and off-label use of commercial human G-CSF (HuG-CSF) is a commonly used treatment for neutropenic animals.
> However, long-term HuG-CSF treatment can be associated with adverse effects, including neutropenia.
> Conversely, all HuG-CSF-treated cats developed neutralizing antibodies, suggesting cross-reactive antibodies to endogenous G-CSF in a majority of the cases with severe neutropenia.
> Strikingly, when PegFeG-CSF was used to rescue cats with HuG-CSF-induced neutropenia, clinically normal neutrophil numbers returned.

**PMID 34509747** · Chi HJ 2021
> Off-label use of a human granulocyte colony stimulating factor (hG-CSF) has been allowed to treat dogs and cats with neutropenia. However, repeated administration of hG-CSF induces undesirable anti-drug antibody (ADA) responses, implying the necessity of animal-derived G-CSF as a therapeutic reagent, preferably with a long-acting capability.

**PMID 27094020** · Pierro J 2016
> Lymphoma was the most common cancer diagnosis; lomustine and vinca alkaloids were the most frequently implicated causative agents.
> Presenting clinical signs included decreased appetite, lethargy, vomiting and diarrhoea.

> All but one cat were treated with intravenous fluids and broad spectrum antibiotics.

**PMID 34967142** · Kim KB 2021
> Two weeks after completion of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor therapy, the cat developed severe thrombocytopenia, with the granulocyte colony-stimulating factor therapy considered to be the most likely cause.
> This is the first reported case of transient severe thrombocytopenia in a cat following granulocyte colony-stimulating factor treatment.

**PMID 39765486** · Dascalu MA 2024
> During January 2022 and September 2024, twenty-two cats diagnosed with feline panleukopenia virus were subjected to filgrastim (Zarzio®, Sandoz, Kundl, Austria) administration at a dose of 6 µg/kg for 3 consecutive days.
> However, red blood cells, haemoglobin, haematocrit and platelets parameters registered a considerable reduction from day 1 to day 5 with a significant statistical difference (p < 0.01), considered as post-administration side effects.

**PMID 41976045** · Tüfekçi E 2026
> Cats were randomly assigned to four groups: low-dose filgrastim (5 µg/kg, n = 13), high-dose filgrastim (20 µg/kg, n = 14), iPPVO (n = 12), and standard supportive treatment (n = 10).
> Survival rates did not differ significantly among groups (p = 0.615), although the high-dose filgrastim group showed the lowest mortality (42.9%).
> However, it should be noted that the efficacy of filgrastim and/or iPPVO treatments has not been definitively confirmed, likely due to the small sample size and the lack of well-controlled randomized studies.

**PMID 38662994** · Klar L 2024
> Only one report on the successful use of filgrastim (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) in cats for severe neutropenia following azathioprine toxicity exists.
> Based on the perceived response to filgrastim in this single feline case report, its use can be considered for the treatment of azathioprine-induced neutropenia in cats.
